Segment and value: who deserves the next dollar

"What is our CAC?" sounds like a question with one answer. It has at least three decisions hidden inside it: what counts as the cost, what counts as a new customer, and which channels get averaged together. Then comes the question the budget meeting actually asks, which is not "what did a customer cost us last quarter" but "what will the next one cost, and will she pay us back?" Tonight reads Kettle & Co's six acquisition channels the way a finance partner would: cost with its base stated, payback on margin rather than revenue, customer value as an input you borrow rather than invent, and the gap between the average customer and the next one.

Part 0

Where we start

Angles session 4 showed that channels bring different customers: paid social's repeat curve flattens at 22 percent while organic search's climbs to 41. Tonight puts money on those curves. Every number comes from one constructed table, Kettle & Co's Q3 2026 acquisition by channel, in pounds. The table is invented and labelled so every time it appears; the arithmetic is the real thing, and it is the same arithmetic you will run on your own company's order table. The marketing lead wants to know where the next quarter's money should go. The data team's job is to put the evidence and its uncertainty on the table. Where the money goes is her call, because she is the one who lives with the result.

Part 1 · covers CAC with its base

A CAC is a fraction, so say what is on top and underneath

5 min live

Customer acquisition cost is spend divided by new customers. The division is trivial; the two words are not. Change what goes on top, media only or fully loaded with fees and staff time, and the number moves. Change what counts underneath, every first order or only first-ever orders with duplicates removed, and it moves again. And add the six channels together into one blended figure and you get a number that is true of none of them.

LiveThe two decisions inside every CAC3 min▶

What goes on top. The narrowest numerator is media spend: what the ad platform invoiced. A fully loaded numerator adds agency and creator fees, tools, and the people who run the channel. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Media-only CAC tells the marketing lead what the auction charges her. Fully loaded CAC tells the finance partner what a customer really costs the business. Organic search shows why the choice matters: it has no media bill at all, so in the constructed table its "spend" is the 4,000 of content cost that produced the pages people found. Leave that out and organic CAC is zero, which flatters it into meaninglessness; put it in and organic CAC is 8.0.

What counts underneath. A "new customer" should mean a first order ever, deduplicated: the same person reached by paid social on Monday and a creator on Wednesday is one new customer, not two. If each channel counts its own "new" from its own reporting, the six denominators add up to more people than actually arrived, and every CAC looks cheaper than it is. The company's own customer table, not the platforms, is where "first order ever" is decided.

So a CAC on a slide should never travel alone. Write it as "30.0 (media only, first-ever orders, deduplicated)" and the next person who compares it with a number from finance will know whether they are comparing like with like.

Self-studyWhy the blended figure keeps winning meetings2 min▶

Blended CAC is easy to compute, stable from quarter to quarter, and it fits on one slide. In the constructed Q3 table it is 64,000 over 2,670 new customers, 24.0. It is a fair number for one question: what did acquiring customers cost the business overall? It is the wrong number for every channel question, because it mixes a channel that costs 8.0 with one that costs 36.0. Worse, it moves when the mix moves even if no channel changes: shift money towards the expensive channels and blended CAC rises with no channel getting worse, the same mix effect Angles session 4 found in blended repeat. Report blended for the overall question, by channel for every allocation question, and say which one you are showing.

Real world

Two CACs for the same quarter. Kettle & Co's marketing lead and finance partner arrive at the quarterly review with different paid social CACs for the same three months. Neither has made a mistake: one divided media spend by first orders in the ad platform's own reporting, the other divided media plus agency fees by first-ever orders from the customer table. Forty minutes of the meeting go on reconciling them. The fix the data team proposes is a one-line definition printed under every CAC on every slide. Constructed example; the two-numerator problem is common wherever marketing and finance report the same cost.

Part 2 · covers payback and margin

Payback is counted in margin, month by month

5 min live

A CAC on its own says what a customer cost, not whether she was worth it. The payback month answers that: the first month in which the cumulative gross margin from a customer covers what it cost to acquire her. Margin, not revenue, because a pound of revenue on tea still has to pay for the tea, the tin, the packing and the postage before it can pay back an ad. A channel that "pays back" on revenue can still be losing money on every customer it brings.

LiveReading the six panels3 min▶

The constructed model is deliberately simple. Every new customer's first order earns 14 of gross margin. Each customer who orders again within six months adds 48 more over that period, spread evenly. So a channel's six-month margin per customer is 14 plus its repeat rate times 48: paid search, with 38 percent repeat, reaches 32.24; paid social, with 22 percent, reaches 24.56.

Organic search pays back at month 0: its CAC of 8.0 is covered by the first order alone. Affiliate pays back at month 1, CAC 15.0 against a first-order margin of 14. Paid search and PR pay back at month 6, just inside the window. Paid social and creators do not pay back within six months at all: paid social is still 5.44 short of its CAC at month 6, creators 7.12 short.

Notice what this does to the part 1 picture. Paid search and paid social had the same CAC, 30.0. On payback they are on opposite sides of the line, and the whole difference is the repeat rate Angles session 4 drew as a cohort curve. Cost tells you half the story; margin over time tells you the other half.

"Not within 6" is not "never". With a longer window, more repeat orders could close the gap. Whether to judge channels on six months, twelve or a lifetime is a finance and marketing choice about how long the business can wait for its money; the data team's job is to show the answer at each window, not to pick one quietly.

Self-studyCustomer value is an input here, not a calculation2 min▶

The six-month margin per customer above is a crude customer value: one window, one average repeat rate, one margin. A proper customer lifetime value (CLV) models how many more orders each customer is likely to place and when, and it has its own methods, assumptions and failure modes. Computing it is taught in Customer Retention, the CLV session ↗.

For allocation work, treat CLV the way you treat a supplier's price: an input with a source and a date. Write down which model produced it, over what horizon, on margin or on revenue, and from which cohort. Then the payback table can use it, and anyone who disagrees with the CLV knows exactly which input to argue with, rather than arguing with the whole recommendation.

Self-studyWhy affiliate's quick payback points back to coupons2 min▶

Affiliate looks excellent on payback: month 1, the fastest of the paid channels. But its six-month repeat is 14 percent, the lowest of any channel, so its six-month margin per customer, 20.72, is the lowest too. It pays back quickly because it is cheap, not because the customers are valuable.

Angles session 3 drew the mechanism that produces exactly this pairing: coupon sites intercepting buyers who were already at checkout, collecting the last click and the commission for an order that was going to happen anyway. A cheap CAC with a weak repeat rate is the signature to check for. If many of affiliate's "new" customers were already on their way, its true CAC is higher than 15.0, because the denominator counts people the channel did not bring. The split of affiliate orders into new and returning customers, and what each did next, is the evidence; what to do with the coupon partners is the BD lead's call.

Real world

The channel that paid back on revenue. A first draft of Kettle & Co's payback table divided cumulative revenue per customer by CAC, and every channel paid back inside three months. The finance partner asked one question: "revenue or margin?" On margin, two channels, paid social and creators, did not pay back within six months at all. The draft was not wrong arithmetic; it answered a question nobody needed answered, because revenue does not pay for an ad until the tea and the postage are paid for. Constructed example; the revenue-for-margin swap is one of the most common errors in payback work.

Part 3 · covers the next dollar

The average customer is not the next customer

5 min live

Every number so far is an average: total spend over total new customers. The marketing lead's question is about the margin: if she puts another 6,000 into paid social next quarter, what does that 6,000 buy? Channels have diminishing returns. The first pounds reach the people most likely to buy; each extra pound reaches people a little less likely, at a little more cost. So the next customer costs more than the average one, and a channel can look healthy on average while its next pound is already losing money.

LiveAverage CAC against marginal CAC3 min▶

Average CAC is total spend over total new customers: for paid social in the constructed Q3, 24,000 over 800, 30.0. Marginal CAC is the extra spend over the extra customers it brings. In the constructed response curve, the next 6,000 on paid social buys 120 new customers: 6,000 over 120, 50.0. The next customer costs two thirds more than the average one.

Put that next to Part 2. At an average CAC of 30.0, paid social was already 5.44 short of paying back by month 6. If the extra 120 customers behaved like the current ones, which is itself an assumption, each would bring about 24.56 of six-month margin against a cost of 50.0. The marketing lead's plan to grow paid social is not obviously wrong: it may be buying reach for the slow brand clock of Angles session 1, or the longer window may close the gap. But "it has the same CAC as paid search" is no longer a reason.

Where does a response curve come from? In practice, from the company's own history of spend changes, from deliberate spend tests, or from a marketing mix model. Each is an estimate with uncertainty, and a curve fitted to a narrow range of past spend says little about spend far outside it. Mix models and spend tests at depth are taught in Marketing Attribution ↗.

LiveData informs the bet; the business places it1 min▶

It is tempting, with a table this clear, to write "move 6,000 from paid social to organic search content" at the bottom and send it. Resist it. The marketing lead knows things the table does not: a product launch that needs reach next quarter, a creator contract already signed, a brand campaign whose payoff will not show in six months. She will also live with the outcome, in her targets and in her next budget round. Whoever lives with the outcome owns the decision. The data team's deliverable is the evidence, laid out as options with their numbers and their uncertainty, by a named date. The choice stays with her.

Self-studySegments beyond channel3 min▶

Channel is only one way to cut "who deserves the next dollar". The same arithmetic, CAC with its base, margin-based payback, average against marginal, applies to any segment that costs something to acquire and earns something afterwards. Two that matter at Kettle & Co are worth working through on your own data:

Business accounts against consumers. Office-tea accounts, the BD lead's business customers, are acquired through trials, samples and sales time rather than ads, and they reorder on a different rhythm. Their CAC numerator is mostly people's time, which is easy to leave out; their repeat pattern looks nothing like a consumer's. Mixing them into consumer CAC distorts both. The BD lead's ask about office-tea trials not converting is ask 24 on the Partnership session 2 triage desk.

Subscribers against one-off buyers. A subscriber's repeat orders are close to scheduled; a one-off buyer's are not. A channel that brings a higher share of subscribers will look better on six-month margin for a structural reason, and the honest comparison splits the channel's customers by what they signed up for.

No constructed numbers are given for these cuts on purpose. Build them from your own customer table and see whether the ranking of your channels survives the split.

Real world

The budget that doubled on an average. At a quarterly review, Kettle & Co's marketing lead proposes growing paid social because its CAC of 30.0 matches paid search. The data team brings one more slide: what the next 6,000 is expected to buy on the constructed response curve, 120 customers at a marginal CAC of 50.0, with a note on how uncertain that curve is. The lead keeps part of the increase for a launch that needs reach, and puts the rest into a spend test so the next quarter's curve rests on evidence rather than one past range. Her decision, made with better numbers. Constructed example; the average-for-marginal swap is how many budgets grow past the point of return.

Build-along

A payback table, and a recommendation that stays a recommendation

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code tonight. You will build Kettle & Co's Q3 payback table by hand from the constructed figures, one column at a time, then write the evidence note the marketing lead takes into her budget decision. This is ask 23 on the Partnership session 2 triage desk, "CAC and payback by channel": owned by data, with marketing and finance consulted. Work the first two steps together, then the rest alone. Do it again on your own company's data afterwards; your numbers will differ.

Homework

Before Angles session 6

30 min
  1. Find your company's CAC on two slides from different teams, and write down each one's numerator and definition of "new". If they differ, write the one-line definition you would print under both.
  2. Build a payback table for three of your channels on margin, at a six-month and a twelve-month window, and note any row whose verdict changes with the window.
  3. Find one channel where a spend change happened in the last year and estimate, roughly and with its uncertainty, what the extra spend bought: your first marginal CAC.
  4. Split one channel's new customers into a segment pair that matters at your company (business against consumer, subscriber against one-off) and see whether its payback verdict survives the split.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Angles session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Kettle & Co's blended CAC is 24.0 (constructed). Why is it the wrong number for deciding which channel gets more money?

Blended CAC answers the overall question, what acquisition cost the business. Allocation is a channel question, and needs each channel's CAC with its numerator and its definition of "new".

2 · Why is payback computed on gross margin rather than revenue?

Margin is what is left to repay the acquisition cost. In the constructed case, paid social and creators do not pay back within six months on margin, which a revenue view hides.

3 · Paid social's average CAC is 30.0, but the next 6,000 buys 120 customers in the constructed response curve. What should the data team put in front of the marketing lead?

The next customer costs more than the average one, and the curve is an estimate. Data informs the bet; the business places it, because the marketing lead lives with the outcome and knows things the table does not.
