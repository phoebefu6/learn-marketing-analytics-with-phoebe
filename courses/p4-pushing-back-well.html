Pushing back well

The ownership map from Partnership session 2 is only useful if somebody can say it out loud to a BD lead who wants a penetration strategy by Friday. Most analysts say it badly in one of two ways: a vague "we'll help" that promises nothing in particular and grows into a takeover, or a flat "that's not our job" that is accurate and lands as a door closing. Tonight is the third way. Every script opens with what the requester gets and when, names the decision it serves, and only then draws the boundary, with the reason. Six scripts for the complaints a data team hears most, an escalation path for when a script is not enough, and the cases where the right answer is simply yes.

Part 1 · covers why vagueness is the unkind option

Clear is kind; a vague yes is not

5 min live

"We'll help with the market research" feels generous. It is the most expensive sentence a data team says. Nobody wrote down what help means, so the requester hears "data is doing the research", the analyst hears "I'll pull a few numbers", and three weeks later both are right and both are angry. A clear answer with a boundary costs a slightly harder minute now and saves the month.

LiveWhy "we'll help" grows3 min▶

An unbounded yes has no edge, so every follow-up lands inside it. "Could you also check the competitors in Japan?" is reasonable once data is "helping with the research", and so is "could you draft the survey?", and so is "could you present it to the board?". Each step is small. On the triage desk, the Japan ask costs 3 modelled hours as a consulted page and 40 if data takes all of it; nobody ever agreed to 40, they agreed to "help".

Resentment arrives from both sides. The analyst feels used, because the owned work is late and nobody seems to notice. The BD lead feels let down, because the research arrived late and was shaped by somebody who has never met a Japanese distributor. Neither of them is wrong, and both were set up by the first sentence.

Self-study"No" is not the opposite of "we'll help"2 min▶

The opposite of a vague yes is not a flat no; it is a specific yes with an edge. On the triage desk, the flat no ("say no to anything not ours") kept the hours low and left nine decisions on anecdote. The specific yes gives the requester something real, on a date, and keeps the part that is theirs with them. It is harder to say than either, which is why it is worth scripting.

Real world

The research project that became a quarter. An analyst agrees to "help with some market research" for a new region. Two months later she is running a survey, buying a panel and writing recommendations, and the dashboard rebuild she owns is a quarter late. Her manager asks why. She does not have an answer that does not sound like an accusation, because nothing was ever written down. Constructed example; the pattern is common.

Part 2 · covers the scripts

Deliverable, date, decision, then the boundary

6 min live

Every script below has the same four parts in the same order. What they get. When. Which decision it serves. Then the boundary, with the reason, which is almost always the same reason: they own the outcome, so they own the decision. The order matters because people hear the first sentence loudest. Open with the boundary and they hear "no"; open with the deliverable and they hear "yes, and here is its shape".

LiveThree scripts for the loudest complaints4 min▶

"Why isn't data doing our market research?" (BD lead, the Japan ask)

★ Script 1 · market research"By day 15 you get one page on our existing Japan orders, their repeat rate and search demand from Japan, so you can brief the research agency on what we already know. The fieldwork and the go or no-go stay with you, because you will own the Japan result."

"Just write our UK supermarket penetration strategy." (BD lead)

★ Script 2 · penetration strategy"By day 18 you get repeat rate, basket and price sensitivity of our current UK online customers by region, so your strategy starts from our own evidence. The strategy itself stays with you: you are the one in the room with the buyers, and you own the outcome."

"Why is data so slow?" (marketing lead)

★ Script 3 · speed"You get the autumn readout on day 16 and the dashboard on day 19. Here is the queue, so you can see what is in front of them. To bring the readout forward I would move the CAC table to next week. Which matters more to you this week? It is your call."
Self-studyThree more: the tracker, the platform number, and "by tomorrow"4 min▶

"Can't data just own the brand tracker?" (brand manager)

★ Script 4 · brand tracker"By day 19 you get sample weighting and a significance read of the agency's wave, so you know which movements are real. The questionnaire and the fieldwork stay with you and the agency, because which brand questions matter is yours to choose."

"Meta says ROAS is 4.1, your number is wrong." (marketing lead)

★ Script 5 · the platform number"By day 12 you get a holdout design for the next Meta flight, and after it runs, a read of what the ads caused, so the budget call rests on that. The 4.1 is Meta's number under Meta's own window; ours will be smaller, and both are honest about what they count."

"We need it by tomorrow." (anyone)

★ Script 6 · by tomorrow"Tomorrow you get the version I can stand behind in a day: orders by channel for the campaign, no new joins. The full readout comes on day 16. If tomorrow's decision cannot wait, tell me what it is, and I will make sure tomorrow's version answers that."

Every date in these scripts is a Kettle & Co working day from the triage desk, constructed. The structure is the part that travels.

Real world

The same boundary, two orders. "The strategy is yours, not ours. We can give you some numbers." and "By day 18 you get repeat, basket and price sensitivity by region, so your strategy starts from our evidence; the strategy stays with you." say the same thing. The first is heard as a refusal and gets escalated. The second is heard as a plan and gets a thank-you. Constructed example; try both on a colleague.

Part 3 · covers escalation and when to say yes

The escalation ladder, and the cases for yes

4 min live

Some pushbacks do not land, and some should not be made. When a script does not settle it, the ladder has three rungs, and every rung arrives with an offer, never only with a complaint. And some asks deserve a plain yes, even when they are inconvenient.

LiveHow to climb a rung without it becoming a fight3 min▶

Rung 1, analyst and requester. The script. Most asks end here, because the requester gets something real on a date.

Rung 2, both leads. When the requester needs more than the menu offers, the data lead and the requester's lead look at the queue together. The data lead arrives with one concrete offer: "we can do the Japan page and the sizing half this month if the dashboard moves to the first week of next month". The point is to make the trade visible, not to win it.

Rung 3, both heads. A shared priority call, in writing, when two fixed dates collide or capacity drops. The triage desk's break button is this rung's case: with one analyst on leave, the right split starves six data-owned asks, and the fix is a re-planned month agreed by both heads before the old dates pass, not a quiet slide into refusing the split asks.

Self-studyWhen saying no is the wrong answer2 min▶

Internal asks are data's job, and "we're busy" is not a boundary, it is a capacity conversation (rung 2). The evidence half of a split ask is data's job too: refusing it is the say-no rung on the desk, which looked efficient and left nine decisions on anecdote. A real emergency (a broken checkout, a campaign sending the wrong price) gets a yes, with what moved said out loud. And when only data can do something, a holdout read, a join of queries to orders, the answer is yes even when the timing is bad, because the alternative is that nobody does it.

The boundary in this whole track is about ownership of the decision, not about effort. Data can work very hard for marketing and still never own the marketing decision.

Real world

The escalation that arrived with an offer. Two fixed dates collide: Black Friday readouts and the budget round. Instead of telling the marketing head "we can't do both", the data lead brings one page: the queue, the two dates, and two options, each saying what would move. The marketing head picks one in five minutes. The same conversation without the page takes an hour and ends with both heads annoyed. Constructed example; the page is the point.

Build-along

Four scripts of your own

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code. Pick the four complaints you hear most, and write one script for each in the four-part order. Then read each one out loud to the person next to you, who plays the requester and says what they heard first. Rewrite until they hear the deliverable before the boundary. Your scripts will differ from the Kettle & Co ones; keep the order.

Step 1 · List your four complaints. In the requester's words, not yours. If you are stuck, start from the six above.

Three questions

Clear all three and Partnership session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The BD lead asks data to write the UK supermarket penetration strategy. Which reply follows the order from Part 2?

Deliverable, date, decision, then the boundary with its reason. A is a door closing, B is the vague yes, C opens with the boundary and has no real date.

2 · Why does a vague "we'll help" usually end in resentment on both sides?

On the triage desk the Japan ask is 3 modelled hours as a consulted page and 40 if data takes all of it. "We'll help" never says which.

3 · Which of these deserves a plain yes, even when the timing is bad?

Internal work only data can do gets a yes; the conversation is about what moves, which is a capacity conversation, not a boundary. The other three are external and belong with brand.
